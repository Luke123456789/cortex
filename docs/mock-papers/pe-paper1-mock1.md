### 13. Mechanical advantage (5)

### 14. The respiratory system and gas exchange (6)

- (a) Trachea → bronchi
- (a) → bronchioles → alveoli
- (b) The walls are thin (one cell thick), so there is a short diffusion distance
- (b) There are many alveoli, giving a large surface area

### 15. The heart, blood and double circulation (5)

- (a) Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume = 150 × 120 = 18 000 ml per minute
- (a) 18 litres per minute
- (b) The left ventricle pumps blood around the whole body (systemic circulation), the right ventricle only to the lungs
- (b) So it must contract with more force to create higher pressure

*In (a), 18 000 not converted scores the first mark only.*

### 19. Types of training (4)

- (a) Continuous training: exercising at a steady (moderate) intensity for a sustained period, at least 20 minutes, with no rest
- (a) Interval training: periods of work (often high intensity) followed by periods of rest or recovery
- (b) A football match involves repeated sprints with periods of recovery (jogging or walking), which interval training copies
- (b) So it develops both the aerobic and anaerobic systems / it is specific to the demands of the game

### 20. Warm up and cool down (3)

- Skill-related or sport-specific practice, e.g. passing drills or practising dodges

*Each mark needs the phase with a suitable example. Static stretches are acceptable for the second phase.*

### 21. Principles of training and FITT (6)

- **Level 3 (5–6):** Accurate knowledge of all three principles, clearly applied to a 1500 m runner, with a well-developed evaluation of the risks of applying them incorrectly. Ideas are well organised.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Knowledge of at least two principles, with some application to the runner and some evaluation of the risks.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Basic knowledge of one or more principles, with little or no application or evaluation.

Indicative content:
- Specificity: training should match the demands of 1500 m, e.g. mainly aerobic with some anaerobic work, running-based, using the muscles and energy systems of the race
- Progressive overload: gradually increase the training load (e.g. distance, speed, or number of intervals) using FITT, so the body adapts
- Reversibility: fitness gains are lost if training stops, e.g. during injury or illness, so the runner should train regularly and keep some activity going
- If training is not specific (e.g. mainly weight training or sprinting), the improvements will not transfer well to the race
- If overload is increased too quickly, there is a risk of overtraining, injury (e.g. shin splints, stress fractures) and fatigue
- If overload is too small or not increased, the runner will plateau and not improve
- A young performer is still growing, so overload needs to be particularly carefully controlled
- Rest and recovery days are needed for adaptation, but too much rest can lead to reversibility
- Varying training can avoid tedium while keeping it specific

*Level 3 needs all three principles applied to the runner and an evaluation of the risks. Definitions alone stay in Level 1.*
