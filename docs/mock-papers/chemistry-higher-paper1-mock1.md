# Chemistry Higher Paper 1: Mock 1

Original questions in the style of AQA GCSE Chemistry (8462) Higher Paper 1 (topics C1–C5). Not a real past paper.

**Time:** 1 hour 45 minutes · **Total:** 100 marks · **Calculator:** allowed

> You may use a calculator. Answer all the questions. Relative atomic masses are given in the questions where you need them. Show your working in calculations: you can get marks for method even if your final answer is wrong. Questions marked 6 marks are extended answers: write in full sentences and organise your ideas. Diagrams are described in words; you can describe any diagram you would draw.

## Questions

### 1. (8 marks)

This question is about magnesium. The atomic number of magnesium is 12.

### 7. (11 marks)

## Mark scheme

### 1. Subatomic particles, isotopes and relative atomic mass (8)

- (a) 12 protons and 12 electrons
- (a) 13 neutrons
- (b) Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
- (c) Multiplies each mass number by its abundance: (24 × 79) + (25 × 10) + (26 × 11) = 2432
- (c) Divides by 100: 2432 ÷ 100 = 24.32
- (c) 24.3
- (d) Magnesium atom: 2,8,2
- (d) Magnesium ion: 2,8

*In (b), 'different mass number' is acceptable for different numbers of neutrons. In (c), 24.32 not rounded scores 2 of the 3 marks.*

### 2. Group 0, Group 1 and Group 7 (8)

- (a)(i) The solution turns orange (or yellow/brown)
- (a)(ii) Correct formulae: Cl₂ + KBr → KCl + Br₂
- (a)(ii) Correctly balanced: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂
- (b) A chlorine atom is smaller / has fewer electron shells than a bromine atom
- (b) So the outer shell is closer to the nucleus and there is a stronger attraction to the nucleus (less shielding)
- (b) So chlorine gains an electron more easily
- (c) Noble gases have a full (stable) outer shell of electrons (8, or 2 for helium)
- (c) So they do not easily lose, gain or share electrons

*In (b), do not credit 'loses electrons more easily' or answers about the reactivity of Group 1. 'Bromine is displaced' alone does not answer (b).*

### 3. The periodic table, metals and non-metals (6)

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A clear, detailed and logically ordered explanation covering how Mendeleev arranged the elements, why he left gaps or changed the order, and why the table was accepted, including the later discovery of predicted elements and/or isotopes.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Some explanation of how the table was arranged and at least one reason it was accepted, with some detail.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about early periodic tables or Mendeleev, with little explanation.

Indicative content:
- Early tables ordered elements only by atomic weight, so some elements were put in groups with the wrong properties, and new elements had no place
- Mendeleev arranged elements mainly by atomic weight, but grouped elements with similar properties together
- He changed the order of some pairs of elements (e.g. tellurium and iodine) so they fitted the properties of their groups
- He left gaps for elements that he thought had not yet been discovered
- He used the gaps to predict the properties of undiscovered elements
- When elements such as gallium and germanium were discovered, their properties matched his predictions, so scientists accepted the table
- Later, the discovery of isotopes and of protons explained why ordering by atomic weight was not always correct: the modern table is ordered by atomic number

*Level 3 needs both how the table was developed and why it was accepted.*

### 4. Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding (8)

- (a) Each sodium atom loses one electron (from its outer shell)
- (a) Each oxygen atom gains two electrons
- (a) Two sodium atoms are needed for each oxygen atom
- (a) Forming Na⁺ and O²⁻ ions, which have full outer shells (the electronic structure of a noble gas)
- (a) Formula Na₂O
- (b)(i) A shared pair of electrons between two atoms
- (b)(ii) Methane is made of small molecules with weak forces between the molecules (intermolecular forces)
- (b)(ii) Little energy is needed to overcome these forces (the covalent bonds do not break when it boils)

*In (a), 'oxygen shares electrons with sodium' scores 0 for the electron transfer marks. In (b)(ii), 'the covalent bonds are weak' or 'the bonds break' scores 0.*

### 5. Small molecules, polymers and giant covalent structures (7)

- (a) Silicon dioxide has a giant covalent structure (lattice)
- (a) There are many strong covalent bonds between the atoms
- (a) A lot of energy is needed to break these bonds
- (b) Poly(ethene) molecules are very large / long chains
- (b) So the intermolecular forces between them are stronger than between small ethene molecules (more energy needed to overcome them)
- (c) C₆₀
- (c) A valid use of nanotubes, e.g. in electronics, to reinforce materials such as tennis rackets, or in nanotechnology

*In (a), any mention of intermolecular forces scores 0 for the second and third marks.*

### 6. Properties of metals and alloys (8)

- (a) Copper atoms are arranged in layers
- (a) The layers can slide over each other
- (b) Brass contains atoms of different sizes (zinc and copper)
- (b) The different sized atoms distort the layers
- (b) So the layers cannot slide over each other as easily
- (c)(i) 1 to 100 nanometres (1 × 10⁻⁹ m to 1 × 10⁻⁷ m)
- (c)(ii) Nanoparticles have a very high surface area to volume ratio
- (c)(iii) A valid risk, e.g. their effects on health are not fully known, they could be breathed in or absorbed, or they could be washed into the environment

*In (a), 'the atoms slide' without layers can score the second mark only. In (b), 'zinc makes it stronger' alone scores 0.*

### 7. Moles, reacting masses and limiting reactants (11)

- (a) Mr of MgO = 40
- (b) Oxygen from the air combines with the magnesium
- (b) The mass of the oxygen that has reacted is added to the mass of the magnesium (the oxygen was not weighed at the start)
- (c) Moles of Mg = 0.36 ÷ 24 = 0.015 mol
- (c) Moles of MgO = 0.015 mol (2 : 2 ratio)
- (c) Mass of MgO = 0.015 × 40 = 0.60 g
- (d) Percentage yield = 0.51 ÷ 0.60 × 100
- (d) 85%
- (e) Moles of Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2 mol and moles of O₂ = 2.4 ÷ 32 = 0.075 mol
- (e) 0.2 mol of Mg needs 0.1 mol of O₂ (2 : 1 ratio)
- (e) There is only 0.075 mol of O₂, so oxygen is the limiting reactant

*Allow error carried forward in (c) to (d). In (e), using Mr = 16 for oxygen gives 0.15 mol and can score the ratio mark, but the conclusion must follow from the student's numbers. 'Oxygen' with no working scores the final mark only.*

### 8. Yield and atom economy (8)

- (a) Total Mr of reactants = 16 + 18 = 34
- (a) Mr of desired product = 3 × 2 = 6, and atom economy = 6 ÷ 34 × 100
- (a) 17.6%
- (b) Less waste is produced
- (b) A second valid reason, e.g. it uses fewer resources, is more sustainable, or is cheaper / more profitable
- (c) Moles of CH₄ = 8.0 ÷ 16 = 0.5 mol
- (c) Moles of H₂ = 0.5 × 3 = 1.5 mol
- (c) Volume = 1.5 × 24 = 36 dm³

*In (a), using 2 instead of 6 for hydrogen (5.88%) can score the first mark only. In (c), allow error carried forward from a wrong number of moles.*

### 9. Reactions of acids, salts and neutralisation (9)

- (a) Zinc chloride
- (b) Correct formulae: Zn + HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
- (b) Correctly balanced: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
- (c) Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
- (c) It is oxidation because zinc loses electrons
- (d) Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid: it is completely ionised in water
- (d) Ethanoic acid is a weak acid: it is only partially ionised
- (d) So hydrochloric acid has a higher concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺), giving a lower pH
- (e) 100 (times)

*In (d), answers saying hydrochloric acid is 'more concentrated' score 0 for that idea: the concentrations are the same. In (c), accept Zn − 2e⁻ → Zn²⁺.*

### 10. Reactions of acids, salts and neutralisation (6)

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A coherent, logically ordered method that would produce pure, dry crystals, including adding excess copper oxide, filtering, and crystallising, with relevant detail and safety.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** The main steps of a method are given, but with some steps missing or out of order, or with little detail; the method would produce crystals but they might not be pure or dry.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple, disconnected statements about making a salt, with little chance of producing crystals.

Indicative content:
- Measure a volume of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker
- Warm the acid gently (to speed up the reaction)
- Add copper oxide a little at a time, stirring, until no more dissolves (excess copper oxide), so all the acid has reacted
- Filter the mixture to remove the excess copper oxide
- Heat the filtrate (copper sulfate solution) in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water, until crystals start to form
- Leave the solution to cool and crystallise
- Remove the crystals, e.g. by filtering, and dry them between filter paper or in a warm oven
- Wear eye protection; avoid boiling the solution dry

*To reach Level 3 the method must use excess copper oxide and filtering before crystallising. Heating to dryness can still reach Level 2.*

### 11. Electrolysis (8)

- (a) Negative electrode: hydrogen
- (a) Positive electrode: chlorine
- (b) Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen
- (b) So hydrogen ions (from the water) are discharged instead of sodium ions
- (c) Correct species: Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + e⁻
- (c) Correctly balanced: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
- (d) Hydrogen ions and chloride ions are removed from the solution as hydrogen and chlorine
- (d) Sodium ions and hydroxide ions are left in the solution

*In (c), accept 2Cl⁻ − 2e⁻ → Cl₂.*

### 12. Exothermic and endothermic reactions and reaction profiles (7)

- (a) Exothermic
- (a) Because the temperature increased (energy is transferred to the surroundings)
- (b) The products are at a lower energy level than the reactants
- (b) The activation energy is the rise from the reactants up to the peak of the curve
- (b) The overall energy change is the drop from the reactants to the products (energy released)
- (c) Energy is needed to break bonds and energy is released when bonds form
- (c) More energy is released making the new bonds than is needed to break the old bonds

*In (a), the reason mark depends on choosing exothermic. In (c), 'breaking bonds releases energy' scores 0 for the first mark.*

### 13. Chemical cells and fuel cells (6)

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A balanced evaluation with several advantages and disadvantages of both, clearly compared, and a reasoned conclusion.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Some advantages and/or disadvantages of both, or a detailed account of one; a conclusion may be missing or unsupported.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about fuel cells or batteries, with little comparison.

Indicative content:
- Fuel cells: the only product is water, so no pollution at the point of use
- Fuel cells keep producing electricity as long as hydrogen is supplied, and refuelling is quick
- Fuel cells do not need to be recharged and do not wear out as quickly as batteries
- Hydrogen is a flammable gas that is difficult to store safely and needs high pressure
- Hydrogen is often made from fossil fuels (e.g. methane) or by electrolysis, which needs energy
- There are few hydrogen filling stations
- Batteries: recharging takes a long time but can be done at home; there are many charging points
- Batteries eventually lose their capacity, and they contain toxic or scarce metals that are hard to dispose of or recycle
- Electricity to recharge batteries may come from fossil fuel power stations
- Conclusion weighing up the points

*Level 3 needs both technologies and a conclusion.*
