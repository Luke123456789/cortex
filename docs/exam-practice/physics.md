## Energy: exam practice

**25 marks · about 30 minutes**

### 1. Energy stores and calculating energy changes (7 marks)

A car of mass 1200 kg is travelling at 15 m/s. The driver brakes and the car stops.  
  
(a) Calculate the kinetic energy of the car before it brakes. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(b) The brake discs have a total mass of 8.0 kg and a specific heat capacity of 450 J/kg °C. Assume all of the kinetic energy is transferred to the brake discs.  
  
change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change  
  
Calculate the temperature rise of the brake discs. Show your working. (3 marks)  
  
(c) Explain why the actual temperature rise of the discs is less than your answer to (b). (2 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- (a) Ek = ½ × 1200 × 15²
- (a) 135 000 J
- (b) 135 000 = 8.0 × 450 × Δθ
- (b) Δθ = 135 000 ÷ 3600
- (b) 37.5 °C (38 °C)
- (c) Some of the energy is transferred to other stores, e.g. the tyres, the road or the air (or by sound)
- (c) The discs transfer energy to the surroundings by heating as they warm up

*In (b), allow an error carried forward from (a).*

### 2. Conservation, dissipation and efficiency (6 marks)

A student wants to find out which of three materials is the best thermal insulator. She has three identical beakers, a kettle, a thermometer, a stopclock and the three materials.  
  
Describe a method the student could use. Include how she would make it a fair test and how she would decide which material is best.  
  
(6 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A clear, logically ordered method that would produce valid results, including what is measured, how it is controlled and how the results are used to reach a conclusion.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** A method with most of the key steps that could produce results, with some idea of how they would be used.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Some relevant steps, but the method would not produce valid results.

Indicative content:
- Wrap each beaker in the same thickness of one of the materials (and leave one unwrapped as a control)
- Pour the same volume of hot water from the kettle into each beaker
- Use water at the same starting temperature for each beaker
- Put a lid on each beaker (made of the same material)
- Measure the temperature at the start and at regular intervals (e.g. every 2 minutes) for the same total time
- Calculate the temperature drop for each material, or plot cooling curves
- The best insulator gives the smallest temperature drop (slowest cooling)
- Take care with hot water

*Level 3 needs control of at least two variables and a clear way of deciding which material is best.*

### 3. Power (6 marks)

An electric motor lifts a 2.0 kg mass through a height of 1.5 m in 3.0 s. The input power to the motor is 25 W.  
  
(a) Calculate the gravitational potential energy gained by the mass. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(b) Calculate the useful power output of the motor. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(c) Calculate the efficiency of the motor. Show your working. (2 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- (a) 29.4 J
- (b) P = E ÷ t = 29.4 ÷ 3.0
- (b) 9.8 W

*Allow an error carried forward from an earlier part.*

## Particle Model of Matter: exam practice

**25 marks · about 30 minutes**

### 1. Density and changes of state (6 marks)

Water can be a solid (ice), a liquid or a gas (steam).  
  
Use the particle model to explain the differences between the three states, and what happens to the particles when ice melts and when water boils.  
  
(6 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A clear, detailed explanation of the arrangement and movement of particles in all three states, linked to density, and of what happens to the particles and their energy during melting and boiling.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Some description of the particles in at least two states and some idea of what happens when a substance changes state.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about solids, liquids or gases, with little explanation.

Indicative content:
- Solid: particles closely packed in a regular arrangement, vibrating about fixed positions
- Liquid: particles close together but randomly arranged, able to move past each other
- Gas: particles far apart, moving quickly in random directions
- Gases are much less dense because the particles are far apart
- When ice melts, energy is used to overcome (break) the forces between the particles, so they can move around
- When water boils, the particles gain enough energy to escape from the liquid completely
- During melting and boiling the temperature stays constant: the energy increases the potential energy (internal energy), not the kinetic energy
- Mass is conserved in a change of state; it is a physical change, so it can be reversed

*Level 3 needs all three states and both changes of state.*

### 2. Internal energy and specific latent heat (7 marks)

A 0.20 kg block of ice at −10 °C is heated until it becomes water at 20 °C.  
  
Specific heat capacity of ice = 2100 J/kg °C  
Specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 334 000 J/kg  
Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J/kg °C  
change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change  
thermal energy for a change of state = mass × specific latent heat  
  
(a) Calculate the energy needed to warm the ice from −10 °C to 0 °C. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(b) Calculate the energy needed to melt the ice at 0 °C. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(c) Calculate the energy needed to warm the water from 0 °C to 20 °C. (1 mark)  
  
(d) Calculate the total energy needed. (1 mark)  
  
(e) Which stage needs the most energy? (1 mark)

**Mark scheme**

- (a) ΔE = 0.20 × 2100 × 10
- (a) 4200 J
- (b) E = mL = 0.20 × 334 000
- (b) 66 800 J
- (c) 0.20 × 4200 × 20 = 16 800 J
- (d) 87 800 J
- (e) Melting the ice

*In (d), allow an error carried forward from (a) to (c).*

### 3. Particle motion in gases and gas pressure (6 marks)

A gas is trapped in a sealed syringe. Its temperature stays constant.  
  
(a) The gas has a pressure of 100 kPa and a volume of 60 cm³. The plunger is pushed in until the volume is 40 cm³.  
  
pressure × volume = constant  
  
Calculate the new pressure. Show your working. (2 marks)  
  
(b) Explain, in terms of particles, why the pressure increases when the volume decreases. (2 marks)  
  
(c) Describe how the motion of the gas particles is related to the temperature of the gas. (2 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- (a) p₂ = 100 × 60 ÷ 40
- (a) 150 kPa
- (b) The particles have less space, so they hit the walls more often
- (b) So there are more collisions per second on each unit area, giving a larger force per unit area (pressure)
- (c) The particles move constantly in random directions
- (c) The higher the temperature, the greater the average kinetic energy (speed) of the particles

*In (b), 'the particles move faster' scores 0: the temperature is constant.*

### 4. Density and changes of state (6 marks)

(a) What is meant by the internal energy of a substance? (2 marks)  
  
(b) A metal block measures 5.0 cm × 4.0 cm × 2.0 cm and has a mass of 0.304 kg. Calculate the density of the metal in kg/m³. Show your working. (3 marks)

## Atomic Structure: exam practice

**25 marks · about 30 minutes**

### 1. Radioactive decay, nuclear radiation and nuclear equations (6 marks)

Compare alpha, beta and gamma radiation.  
  
Your answer should include what each type of radiation is, how ionising it is and how penetrating it is.  
  
(6 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A detailed comparison of all three types covering what each is, ionising power and penetration (with typical absorbers or range in air), with clear comparative statements.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** A description of at least two types with some comparison of ionising power or penetration.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about one or more types of radiation.

Indicative content:
- Alpha: two protons and two neutrons (a helium nucleus)
- Beta: a high-speed electron from the nucleus
- Gamma: electromagnetic radiation (a wave) from the nucleus
- Alpha is the most ionising, beta less, gamma the least
- Alpha is the least penetrating: stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air
- Beta is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium
- Gamma is the most penetrating: only reduced by thick lead or concrete
- The more ionising the radiation, the less penetrating it is

*Level 3 needs all three types and both ionising and penetrating power.*

2 h: 849 Bq

This question is about nuclear energy and radiation.

## Magnetism and Electromagnetism: exam practice

**25 marks · about 30 minutes**

### 1. Induced potential, generators and transformers (6 marks)

A moving-coil microphone contains a coil of wire attached to a thin diaphragm. The coil is around one pole of a permanent magnet.  
  
Explain how the microphone produces an electrical signal from sound waves.  
  
(6 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A clear, logically ordered explanation linking the sound wave to the movement of the coil, the induced potential difference and how the signal matches the sound.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Some explanation of how movement of the coil in the magnetic field produces a potential difference.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about sound, coils or magnets, with little explanation.

Indicative content:
- Sound waves are pressure variations that make the diaphragm vibrate
- The coil, attached to the diaphragm, moves backwards and forwards with it
- The coil moves in the magnetic field of the magnet
- This induces a potential difference across the coil (the generator effect)
- If the coil is part of a complete circuit, an induced current flows
- The direction of the induced pd reverses each time the coil changes direction, so it is alternating
- Louder sounds give larger movements and a larger pd; higher frequencies give a higher frequency signal
- So the electrical signal has the same pattern (frequency and amplitude) as the sound wave

*Level 3 needs the coil moving in the field, an induced pd, and the signal matching the sound.*

### 2. Electromagnetism and the motor effect (7 marks)

A wire of length 0.20 m carries a current of 2.5 A. It is at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.15 T.

### 1. Red-shift and the Big Bang (6 marks)

Explain how observations of light from distant galaxies support the Big Bang theory, and what more recent observations suggest about the universe.  
  
(6 marks)

**Mark scheme**

- **Level 3 (5–6):** A clear explanation of red-shift, how it shows the universe is expanding and supports the Big Bang, plus the more recent evidence from supernovae and what it suggests.
- **Level 2 (3–4):** Some explanation of red-shift and how it supports the Big Bang, possibly without the more recent observations.
- **Level 1 (1–2):** Simple statements about galaxies, red-shift or the Big Bang.

Indicative content:
- Light from distant galaxies is red-shifted: its wavelength is longer than expected
- This means the galaxies are moving away from us
- The further away a galaxy is, the greater its red-shift, so the faster it is moving away
- So the whole universe is expanding
- If it is expanding, it must once have been much smaller: it began from a very small, extremely hot and dense region (the Big Bang)
- Since 1998, observations of supernovae suggest that distant galaxies are moving away faster and faster: the expansion is accelerating
- Scientists suggest this is caused by dark energy, and there may be dark mass too
- These are not yet understood, so the theory may change as new evidence is found

*Level 3 needs red-shift linked to expansion and the Big Bang, and at least one more recent observation.*
